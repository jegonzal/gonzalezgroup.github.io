Long about 200 words

Joseph E. Gonzalez is a professor in the Department of Electrical Engineering and Computer Sciences at UC Berkeley. His research, at the intersection of machine learning and computer systems, studies how new systems abstractions can enable new forms of machine learning and AI.

As a PhD student in machine learning at Carnegie Mellon, he developed GraphLab and PowerGraph for large-scale graph computation. He co-founded Turi (formerly GraphLab), which Apple acquired. As a postdoc in Berkeley's AMPLab, he led GraphX, now part of Apache Spark. At Berkeley, his group studied the design of systems for serving and tuning machine learning models, including Clipper for prediction serving and Ray Tune for hyperparameter tuning, and Simon Mo developed Ray Serve from Clipper before becoming a PhD student advised by Gonzalez. The group then moved to infrastructure for large language models. His former student Lianmin Zheng created SGLang together with Ying Sheng and contributed to vLLM and Chatbot Arena. Simon Mo later co-founded Inferact, the company built around vLLM. His former students Shishir Patil and Tianjun Zhang led Gorilla, and Charles Packer, Sarah Wooders and Kevin Lin created MemGPT. Gonzalez's current research spans AI agents, post-training and continual learning, and neural architecture innovation.

He is a founding member of Berkeley's Sky Computing Lab, LMSYS and RISELab. His honors include a VLDB Test of Time Award for GraphLab, the ACM SIGMOD Systems Award for Apache Spark (shared) and an NSF CAREER Award.

Research philosophy

Gonzalez shapes his research agenda around one question: “If we solve today's hot problems, what new problems will those solutions create?” That question led him to machine-learning systems in 2008, when most researchers were developing new models. It led him to model serving and inference systems in 2015, as the field focused on scaling training. Today he is betting that AI agents will become highly capable and widespread. He studies how systems must change for thousands of such agents, how people can steer and negotiate through them, and how models can move from learning from data to learning from teachers.
